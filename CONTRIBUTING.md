# Contributing

Help readers understand the costs, markets, adoption, and economic effects of AI.

## Suggest a resource

1. Check whether the resource or an earlier version is already listed.
2. Choose the most specific existing subtopic. Propose a new one when it serves a distinct economic question.
3. Use the original author, institution, publisher, or dataset link where possible.
4. Add a short description of what the reader will learn.
5. Add the author or organization, year, and source label. Mark changing dashboards and pricing pages as `Living`.
6. State access limits when known. Disclose your connection to the resource in your submission.

## Entry format

```markdown
- [Resource title](https://example.org/resource) - Author or organization (year). One or two sentences explaining the economic question and useful content. `Study`
```

Use the labels defined in the README. A `Study` label does not imply peer review. Identify working papers and report later publication when verified.

## Selection criteria

- Direct relevance to an economic question about AI.
- Clear authorship and a stable source link.
- Enough substance to help readers learn, compare evidence, or do analysis.
- A clear contribution beyond resources already listed.
- Descriptions that distinguish observation, model assumptions, forecasts, and opinion.

Prioritize useful research, datasets, and analysis scattered across different sources. Exclude routine company filings, annual reports, investor-relations archives, and recurring corporate sustainability disclosures. Analysis that uses filings can qualify when it adds a clear economic explanation, comparison, or compiled dataset.

Include industry analysis when it provides a useful framework or evidence. Label the affiliation and date. Keep generic AI tool catalogs, affiliate lists, promotional launch posts, and unrelated machine-learning tutorials out of the main list.

## Editing and verification

Open the source and check its title, authorship, date, and fit with the annotation. A working link alone does not verify a claim. Check for revisions, corrections, retractions, and linked follow-up work. Prefer the current landing page over a temporary download URL.

Use one main location per resource and internal cross-links where useful. Do not duplicate a paper under its old and new titles. Avoid unsupported statistics, promises of returns, and claims that task exposure equals job displacement.

Keep descriptions short, factual, and in plain language. Explain technical terms when needed. Do not use em dashes or contractions.

## Maintenance

When reviewing an entry, update its annotation if its title, evidence, access, or publication status changed. Review living sources more often than foundational papers. Update the README review date only after an editorial review, not after an automated link check.

Link to third-party resources without copying their full text, figures, or datasets. Their own licenses and access terms continue to apply.
